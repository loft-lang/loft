pub fn seg_curvature(t: Track, i: integer) -> float

Signed curvature: + turning left (centre on the left of travel), - turning right.

The same left-positive convention track_offset uses, which is why the two are checked against each other: on a turn signed positive, the positive offset is the INSIDE rail.

pub fn offset_legal(t: Track, d: float) -> boolean

I-CURV — an offset degenerates when |d| >= R. This IS the minimum curve radius, and it falls out of the geometry rather than being a tuning constant.

|d|, not d, because a way has BOTH rails: the outer one is never in trouble and the inner one collapses at the tightest turn anywhere on the track. So the question is "may this WAY be this wide", and past the answer track_offset still returns a Track — one whose inner arc has a NEGATIVE length and is no longer equidistant.

pub fn track_offset(t: Track, d: float) -> Track

The parallel track at distance d (left normal positive). For straights and arcs the offset is again a straight / a concentric arc, so equidistance is EXACT here — the only cost anywhere is flattening, err ~ c^2/(8R).

EQUIDISTANCE DOES NOT PICK A SIDE, and that is the trap this function fell into. A left offset of a turn moves TOWARDS the centre, so its radius SHRINKS by d; only a right offset grows. Reading the sweep direction the other way up puts every arc on the far side of the way from its straights — still exactly d from the centreline, still passing an equidistance gate, and no longer JOINED. The number that sees it is the gap at a joint, not the distance to the line.

Which segment of the track is nearest this point? This is the P3 arbitration rule in embryo: nearest-surface-wins, ties to the lower index — deterministic, order-free.

pub fn way_mark(t: Track, halfwidth: float, s: HexSet)

MARK ONLY — phase 1 of the two-phase build.

P3 lesson: marking cells and cutting edges must NOT be interleaved per part. If each part cuts its own boundary as it lands, a later part can turn an already-blocked edge into an interior one — and that stale edge is never cleared, so the result depends on which part ran first. Mark every part, THEN cut once.

A cell is marked on its CENTRE, so the footprint quantises and halfwidth does not survive the trip: between two rings of centres, every width gives the same cells.

pub fn cut_arb(s: HexSet, e: EdgeSet, sf: Surfaces, nsurf: integer, mat: integer)

CUT ONCE — phase 2. For each boundary edge of the FINAL footprint, pick the nearest surface among ALL registered ones (ties to the lower id). Order-independent by construction: it never looks at what was stamped when, only at the finished cells.

pub fn way_stamp(t: Track, halfwidth: float, s: HexSet, e: EdgeSet,

Mark the way's footprint into s, cut its boundary into e, and tag every blocked edge with the surface of the NEAREST segment. One walk, one source of truth.

For ONE way. Calling it once per part of a multi-part way is the interleaving the phase-1 note above forbids — same cells, walls left standing inside the finished footprint, and their owner decided by the stamping order.

pub fn way_steps(t: Track, s: HexSet, f: Heights, rise: float, tread: float)

A STAIRCASE: every tread of travel along the way raises the ground by rise.

The same routine is a terraced hillside, or a ramp at rise = 0. What makes it a good test of the whole model is that the tread is quantised by the CELL ROWS, and the row spacing depends on the direction of travel (the 24-direction tiers) — so a tread that is clean along one heading skips whole steps along another, which shows up as a DOUBLE RISER between adjacent cells. That is a real defect (a trip hazard), and it is what sets the minimum tread per direction.

The number: a flight's shortest safe tread is the spacing of its own cells along the way — sqrt(3) = 1.732 down a row, 1.5 down a column. There is no tread that is fine enough for every heading.
