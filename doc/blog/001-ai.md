# Building a stable language with the use of AI

Building a project with AI agents is not so different from running a project staffed by a lot
of eager but inexperienced programmers who google a lot.

They are eager: they never tire, they work through the night, and they will happily take on
anything you point them at. They are inexperienced: they do not take the time to find where a
problem really comes from, so they fix exactly the problem in front of them and miss the fix
that was actually needed. And they google a lot: they know a great deal about programming in
general and nothing about *your* project, unless your project tells them.

None of that is new. Every team lead has managed people like this. What is new is having a
whole team of them, at once, for the price of a subscription. This post is about what that has
meant for loft, the language I am building, and what I had to put in place to make it work.

## Loft is not a new language

Loft had already been in development for eight years before any AI was involved. It was not
public; it was mine. So the agents did not invent the language. They inherited one, with its
own ideas about memory, data and types, and they had to work inside them.

What changed was not only the speed. The bigger change is how *complete* the language has
become. An agent can take another language — Julia, OCaml, Python, Lua — and turn its idioms
into small loft programs that either run, or are refused with a clear message. Every program
that fails is a question: should loft say this? Some answers became features; multiple
dispatch came out of the comparison with Julia. Others became recorded decisions not to.
And some turned out to be bugs. Re-running the OCaml comparison this week found two programs
that gave a wrong answer without any warning, and both were filed the same day.

I would never have had the patience to do that comparison by hand, language after language.
An eager team does not mind.

## What I actually do

I write very little of the code myself now. My work is mostly steering: guiding the agents
around problems, in many different ways, and devising ways of working that make the most of
what they are good at while covering for what they cannot see.

Their strong parts are easy to list. They are fast, they do not tire, they know a lot, and
they follow a written method faithfully once they have read it. Their blind spots are just as
clear. They remember nothing between sessions. They rush to a fix. They trust their own
explanation. And they bring in what is true in general when it is not true here.

So most of my effort goes into the project's *working methods* rather than its code. Each
blind spot gets a habit, a document or a tool that makes it harmless, and each strength gets
room to run. Day to day it looks small: "are there more places with the same problem?",
"you are going too fast, write a test that compares first", "take the next task". But every
correction that keeps coming back gets written into the project, so the next session starts
with it. The sections below are those blind spots, one by one, and what we put in their way.

## Challenge one: nobody remembers anything

An agent starts every session with no memory. It does not remember yesterday's work, the
design discussion from last month, or why a strange-looking piece of code is the way it is.

So everything that needs to be remembered has to be *in the project*. Every bug, feature,
formal rule and design decision carries a tag, like `@F7` or `@C79`, so that it can be found
by a search and tested again. The project's documentation is not a nice extra; it is the
team's memory.

That memory goes stale, just like a person's. The document that explains how the project
survives without any single person in it claimed the docs were about 73,000 lines. When I
counted while writing this post, they were about 352,000. Nobody lied; the number was true
once and nobody updated it. The rule we now follow is to never write down a number that a
command can produce — write down the command.

But none of this is unique to AI. A team of people that grows its knowledge needs exactly the
same discipline. The difference is that people hide the problem for a while by remembering
things. Agents cannot, so the gaps show up immediately.

## Challenge two: the exact problem is fixed, the real one is not

This is where inexperience shows most. An agent finds a bug, finds a change that makes the
failing case pass, and stops. The exact problem is fixed. But it never took the time to ask
where the problem comes from, so the real fix — the one place that, once corrected, fixes
this case and every case like it — is missed. The same mistake keeps living in five other
places. Over time this creates many slightly different versions of the same logic, each one
patched for its own bug, with no overall design holding them together.

It shows up even more clearly in optimisation. An interpreted program ran five times slower
than the Python original. The profiler pointed at one comparison, a character against a
literal, which took seven interpreter steps. The first idea was a special instruction for
exactly that comparison. That would have made the profiled line faster, and nothing else: a
fix for the exact problem. Looking for the source instead found two different things. The
compiler converted the literal back and forth on every comparison, which was fixed for every
program at once. And the biggest cost was not in the comparison at all: an environment
variable was read on every call into compiled library code. Reading it once removed it.

Optimisation has a particular temptation: write a fast special case in Rust for the slow
routine, and the benchmark is green. Every one of those is a narrow fix — the routine is fast,
and every user's own loop of the same shape stays slow. Loft's rule is that a slow library
routine is work for the compiler, never a hand-written shortcut, and each shortcut that does
exist is listed with the condition for removing it.

The fix for inexperience is the same as with people: make taking the time cheaper than not
taking it. Before an agent fixes something non-trivial, it builds a small table of cases
around the bug, varying one thing at a time, to see where the real boundary is. Our working
rule puts it bluntly: *the urge to fix is the signal you have not earned it yet.*

The second guard is a set of *formal rules*: short, precise statements of how the
language must behave. The rules come first, and code that disagrees with a rule is the thing
that gets fixed. Every place in the code that enforces a rule names it, so "where is this
rule implemented?" becomes a search.

The rules only help if they are checked constantly. A rule chapter that says "zero open
deviations" is a claim, and a claim has to be measured again. We have had chapters reading
zero for weeks while a real bug in exactly that area was open, because the tests behind the
rule never varied the one thing that mattered.

The upside is real, though. When a bug comes in, the rules say what the right answer is, so
there is much less discussion about what to implement. And the logic is easier to find. One
month, 22 bugs came in on the same kind of collection. Almost all of them were cases where the
rules already gave the right answer, which pointed at one piece of logic that had been
written out three times and extended separately by three earlier fixes — each one fixing its
exact problem. Fixing that one place fixed the class.

## Challenge three: the tests have problems of their own

Agents write tests, a lot of them. Loft now has around two thousand test programs, on top of
the Rust test suites. That is good, but the tests become a system that needs managing.

- **Time.** A full test run takes long enough that it has to be planned. Running everything
  after every change is not possible, so we built tools to run only the tests a change can
  affect, and to run the full set once per change whose reach we cannot predict.
- **Memory.** A single bug once made one test allocate almost 60 GB in seconds, and the
  operating system killed two other agents' sessions to recover. Tests now run with a memory
  ceiling, and when they hit it, they say which type filled the memory.
- **Disk.** To prove that a test can actually catch the bug it was written for, we build the
  old, broken version and run the test against it. Each of those builds costs about 2 GB of
  disk. Multiply by several agents working in parallel, and disk space becomes a daily topic.

A test that cannot fail is worse than no test, because it makes you feel safe. So every new
test records the old build it was checked against. Eager programmers write a lot of tests;
someone has to check that the tests are testing something.

## The upside

With all those challenges, why do it? Because the result is worth it.

- **Work happens while I sleep.** Several agents work through the night on different parts of
  the language at once — say, one on performance, one on the type system, one on the
  libraries.
- **Bugs are found by checking rules, not by waiting for users.** Most bugs are now found by
  validating the formal rules, or by comparing loft against other languages, before anyone
  runs into them in a real program.
- **The turnaround is fast.** A bug is often fixed within the hour of being found, with a test
  that proves it. That is hard to match without AI.
- **The cost is low.** I pay about 200 euro a month. Before, getting the base language into
  good shape meant taking half a year of sabbatical. Measured against that, it is cheap.

## What I would tell someone starting

Treat the agents like a team of eager, inexperienced programmers who google a lot, and treat
your own job as designing how that team works. Do not expect them to remember, so make the
project remember for them. Do not expect them to look for the source of a problem, so make
looking cheaper than skipping it, and write the design down as rules they can check against.
Do not trust their explanations, so give them tools that can prove an explanation wrong. And
do not trust your own documentation either: measure it again.

That is not really advice about AI. It is advice about leading a team. The AI just makes it
impossible to skip.
